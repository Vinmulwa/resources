Overview

The most demanding seat in the organisation

Mid-level managers translate senior strategy into operational results while staying close enough to the front line to understand how work really gets done. In a growing organisation that seat is more complex than ever.

🔗

Problems rarely respect boundaries

When a critical resource runs low, the cause could be failures, delays, uneven distribution, slow repairs, poor visibility or demand outpacing capacity. No single department owns the whole answer, so solving it takes judgement, alignment and an understanding of how decisions move.

🧭

The role is to connect, not just supervise

A mid-level manager links strategy, people, technical systems and business performance. Three closely related capabilities make that possible.

1 Strategic leadership

Connecting daily work to business outcomes

Strategic leadership is understanding what the organisation is trying to achieve, identifying the factors that matter most and directing effort towards measurable results. You may not set the strategy, but you make it operational.

Think beyond departmental boundaries

Every team is measured on something different, yet the business needs one thing: the whole system working. A department can hit its own targets while the organisation still underperforms. A workshop may complete many repairs, but if the repaired items never reach the right locations, availability stays low.

  1. What business outcome are we trying to improve?
  2. What is preventing that outcome?
  3. Which departments influence the result?
  4. What trade-offs might our proposed solution create elsewhere?
  5. How will we know whether the intervention worked?
The shift

Move the focus from departmental activity to overall system performance.

Understand the economics behind technical decisions

Technical choices have financial consequences: asset wear drives replacement spend, poor coordination creates downtime and needless movement, and inventory is a balancing act between tied-up capital and prolonged repairs. You do not need to be an analyst, but you should read the link between performance and cost.

Cutting repair spend is not an improvement if it drives up repeat failures or downtime.

Prioritise using evidence

Scaling organisations generate many competing demands at once. Treating everything as urgent leads to reactive management. Weigh each issue against these factors:

SafetyRegulatory compliance Impact on core operationsFinancial exposure Operational urgencyDownstream processes affected

Usually deserves priority

  • A safety-critical fault
  • A recurring issue affecting several sites
  • Work that unblocks other teams

Should not crowd out the important

  • A routine administrative improvement
  • An isolated low-impact incident
  • Emergencies that displace non-urgent but vital work

Make priorities explicit, explain your reasoning and protect important but non-urgent work from being displaced by constant emergencies.

2 Stakeholder management

Getting different teams to work towards the same outcome

Stakeholder management is understanding the priorities, responsibilities and constraints of everyone whose work or decisions affect your objectives. It does not mean pleasing everyone. It means aligning people around shared outcomes while managing legitimate differences.

💰

Finance

Cost control and discipline.

⚙️

Engineering

Technical reliability and standards.

🚚

Operations

Availability and day-to-day delivery.

📦

Procurement

Supplier lead times and purchasing procedures.

Same company goals, different immediate pressures. Your job is to see all of them.

Manage upwards

Senior leaders need visibility, reliable information, clear recommendations and confidence that risks are under control. Never present a problem without its business impact and a proposed way forward. When escalating, communicate four things:

Step 1

Situation

What happened, and what evidence supports it?

Step 2

Impact

How does it affect availability, cost, safety, customers or growth?

Step 3

Proposed action

What do you recommend, and why?

Step 4

Decision required

What approval, resource or intervention do you need?

Instead of…

  • “Items are piling up at the workshop.”

Say…

  • How many await repair and the main failure categories
  • The effect on service availability
  • The expected recovery timeline
  • The resources or decisions needed
Bad news travels best early

Delayed escalation can turn a manageable problem into a major disruption. Be concise, factual and solution-oriented, and make clear what is information, what you can do independently and what needs their authority.

Work laterally with fellow managers

Build the relationship before the crisis

Many problems cross departments and no single manager controls the whole process. Learn your peers’ targets and constraints, share useful information and raise dependencies before they become urgent.

Debate the process, not the person

Rather than “they are delaying my work”, find where the delay occurs, why, who owns the next step and what change would improve the whole process. Do not promise dates on another team’s behalf, and treat shared responsibilities as explicit commitments with owners and deadlines, not favours.

Lead technical teams with clarity and respect

Specialists often hold detailed practical knowledge that managers lack. Respect it, while holding firm on standards and results. Explain why a task matters, and make it safe to report recurring faults, unsafe conditions, missing tools, poor-quality parts and unrealistic deadlines. Before blaming people, diagnose the real problem:

Skills gap

People lack the know-how.

→ Train and coach

Resource constraint

Tools, parts or time are missing.

→ Remove the blocker

Unclear procedure

The way of working is ambiguous.

→ Define the standard

Performance problem

Expectations are clear and resourced, yet unmet.

→ Give direct feedback

Low output? Check workload, equipment, parts, complexity and quality requirements before concluding the team is underperforming. Set clear expectations for safety, workmanship, documentation and escalation; give constructive feedback; recognise good work; and make sure frontline observations reach those who can act.

3 Organisational dynamics

Understanding how decisions really happen

Beyond the org chart sit informal influence, competing priorities, trust, reputation and unwritten expectations. In a business with a head office and regional offices, some decisions are made locally while others need approval from elsewhere, and visibility into local realities varies. Understand all of this without being consumed by politics.

Three ideas to keep apart

Accountability

What outcome must I deliver?

The result you are expected to answer for.

Authority

What can I decide and control?

The decisions and resources formally in your hands.

Influence

Who must enable the outcome?

People whose support you need even though they do not report to you.

When responsibility exceeds authority: clarify decision rights and dependencies early. Agree what you can decide, what needs consultation, what needs approval and how unresolved issues escalate. Do not silently absorb outcomes that depend on others, but never hide behind limited authority either. Act on what you can, build support, and escalate the specific decisions that remain.

Bridge head office and local reality

Head office

Sets strategic direction, technical standards, budgets and growth priorities. Needs accurate information about what is happening locally.

Local teams

Execute and respond to local conditions. Need clarity about strategic expectations and approval requirements.

When an instruction meets a local constraint, do not frame it as “head office versus us”. Explain the operational reality, bring evidence and propose practical options. If a delayed delivery of imported inputs threatens an assembly target, share the affected stages, stock position, lead times, consequences and alternatives, so leaders decide early rather than hearing about missed targets late. Respect reporting lines, but never let them block necessary coordination. Where reporting lines overlap, agree priorities and clarify who has the final say.

Build credibility through consistency

Deliver on commitmentsShare accurate informationAcknowledge mistakesGive credit fairlyAvoid public blame
Separate facts from interpretations

A missed deadline is observable. Deliberate obstruction is an assumption. Investigate the cause before drawing conclusions, and address disagreements directly with the people involved. Organisational awareness is not manipulation. It is understanding context, anticipating consequences and working constructively within the system with integrity.

4 In practice

One operational challenge, three lenses

Imagine availability of a critical resource is declining across a large network. The reflex is to tell one team to produce more. A stronger manager examines the entire cycle the resource moves through.

🎯

Strategic leadership

Frames the objective: increase usable availability while controlling costs and protecting asset life.

🤝

Stakeholder management

Brings technicians, operations, logistics, software and engineering together to find where the resource leaks out of productive service.

🗺️

Organisational awareness

Identifies who controls priorities, repair capacity, movements, data and spending approvals, and which actions need sign-off.

Then run the loop

Baseline

Establish where things stand today.

Bottlenecks

Identify the principal constraints.

Owners

Assign clear responsibilities.

Review

Track progress against agreed measures.

It works everywhere

The same approach applies to assembly and delivery programmes. Output depends not only on team productivity but on input availability, documentation, quality assurance, tooling, training, system configuration and coordination across procurement, engineering and operations. The manager’s value is making those dependencies visible and keeping every function pulling towards one delivery target.

Conclusion

Become the link between strategy and execution

You do not need the highest technical authority or to make every big decision. Your effectiveness comes from understanding the operating system, asking the right questions, building productive relationships and making sure responsibilities become results.

Trusted by leadersfor sound judgement.
Trusted by peersfor dependable collaboration.
Trusted by teamsfor fair, informed leadership.
The defining skill of a mid-level manager is turning complex, cross-functional challenges into clear priorities, coordinated action and measurable results.
