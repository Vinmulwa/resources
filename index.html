Introduction: Leadership from the middle

Mid-level management is where organisational strategy meets operational reality.

Mid-level management occupies one of the most demanding positions in an organisation. Managers at this level are expected to translate senior management's strategy into operational results while remaining close enough to frontline teams to understand the technical and practical realities of execution.

In a scaling electric mobility business, this responsibility is particularly complex. Fleet availability depends on the coordinated performance of batteries, motorcycles, swapping stations, software systems, electrical infrastructure, mechanical maintenance, logistics and field operations. A problem that appears to belong to one department can quickly affect several others.

The mid-level manager's role is therefore not simply to supervise tasks. It is to connect strategy, people, technical systems and business performance. This requires three closely related capabilities: strategic leadership, stakeholder management and the ability to navigate organisational dynamics.

1. Strategic leadership: Connecting daily work to business outcomes

Strategic leadership is the ability to understand what the organisation is trying to achieve, identify the factors that matter most and direct effort towards measurable results.

A mid-level manager may not determine company strategy, but they play a crucial role in making that strategy operational. If senior management wants to expand the fleet, increase revenue, improve customer experience or reduce operating costs, the manager must understand what those objectives mean for their team and for the wider operating system.

Think beyond departmental boundaries

In an electric mobility operation, engineering and operations are interdependent. Battery technicians may be measured on repair output, station teams on availability, motorcycle technicians on repair turnaround, and software teams on system performance. Yet the business ultimately needs motorcycles that are ready to work, batteries available where riders need them, and infrastructure that supports reliable operations.

A department can achieve its own targets while the business still underperforms. For instance, a workshop might complete many battery repairs, but if repaired batteries are not transported to the right stations, fleet availability may remain low.

A strategic manager therefore asks:

  • What business outcome are we trying to improve?
  • What is preventing that outcome?
  • Which departments influence the result?
  • What trade-offs might our proposed solution create elsewhere?
  • How will we know whether the intervention worked?

This shifts the focus from departmental activity to overall system performance.

Understand the economics behind technical decisions

Technical decisions have financial consequences. Battery degradation affects replacement expenditure and asset life. Poor charging coordination increases downtime and may create unnecessary battery movements. Motorcycle failures reduce productive hours and can increase maintenance costs. Excessive spare-parts inventory ties up working capital, while insufficient stock prolongs repairs.

Managers do not need to become financial analysts, but they should understand the relationship between technical performance and business economics.

  • Fleet and battery availability.
  • Motorcycle and battery downtime.
  • Mean time to repair and repair turnaround time.
  • Repeat failures and first-time fix rates.
  • Charging utilisation and charging-related delays.
  • Maintenance cost per motorcycle or kilometre.
  • Battery life, degradation and replacement trends.
  • Spare-parts availability and inventory exposure.

These indicators should be interpreted together. For example, reducing repair expenditure is not necessarily an improvement if it increases repeat failures or downtime.

Prioritise using evidence

Scaling businesses generate many competing demands. Managers may face urgent motorcycle repairs, battery shortages, station expansion, CKD assembly issues, software defects and requests from senior leadership simultaneously.

Treating every issue as equally urgent leads to reactive management. Prioritisation should consider safety, regulatory compliance, fleet impact, financial exposure, operational urgency and the number of downstream processes affected.

A safety-critical electrical fault, for example, must take precedence over a routine administrative improvement. A recurring battery failure affecting several stations may deserve more attention than an isolated low-impact incident.

The manager's responsibility is to make priorities explicit, communicate the reasoning and ensure that important but non-urgent work is not continually displaced by emergencies.

2. Stakeholder management: Getting different teams to work towards the same outcome

Stakeholder management is the ability to understand the priorities, responsibilities and constraints of the people whose decisions or work affect your objectives.

In a multi-department and multi-country electric mobility company, stakeholders include senior management, country leadership, fellow mid-level managers, battery and motorcycle technicians, assembly and fabrications teams, software engineers, electrical and mechanical engineers, procurement,house keeping teams, admins, logistics, finance, suppliers and station operations teams.

These stakeholders may share the same company objectives but have different immediate pressures. Finance may prioritise cost control, engineering may prioritise technical reliability, operations may prioritise fleet availability, and procurement may prioritise supplier lead times and purchasing procedures.

Effective stakeholder management does not mean pleasing everyone. It means aligning people around shared outcomes while managing legitimate differences.

Manage upwards: Working effectively with senior management

Senior management needs visibility, reliable information, clear recommendations and confidence that operational risks are being managed. A mid-level manager should avoid presenting problems without explaining their business impact or proposing a way forward.

When escalating an issue, communicate four things:

1

The situation: What has happened, and what evidence supports it?

2

The impact: How does it affect fleet availability, cost, safety, customers or expansion?

3

The proposed action: What do you recommend, and why?

4

The decision required: What approval, resource or intervention is needed from senior management?

For example, rather than reporting that batteries are accumulating at the workshop, explain the number awaiting repair, the principal failure categories, the effect on station availability, the expected recovery timeline and the resources or decisions needed.

Be concise, factual and solution-oriented. Senior management should be able to distinguish between information you are providing, action you can take independently and decisions that require their authority.

It is equally important to communicate bad news early. Delayed escalation can turn a manageable operational problem into a major business disruption.

Work laterally: Building relationships with fellow managers

Fellow mid-level managers are often your most important partners because many operational problems cross departmental boundaries.

Battery availability, for example, may depend on utility provider reliability, battery technician capacity, station charging capacity, swap station customer traffic or duty cycle or station-level demand and battery operations logistics. No single manager controls the entire process.

Develop relationships before a crisis occurs. Understand your colleagues' targets, constraints and decision-making responsibilities. Share information that helps them perform their work, and raise dependencies before they become urgent.

When disagreements arise, focus on the process and the business outcome rather than personalities. Instead of saying that another department is delaying your work, establish where the delay occurs, why it occurs, who owns the next step and what change would improve the overall process.

Avoid promising delivery dates on behalf of another team without confirming its capacity and agreement. Equally, avoid treating cross-functional support as a favour. Where teams have shared responsibilities, establish explicit commitments, owners and deadlines.

Lead the technical workforce through clarity and respect

Battery technicians, motorcycle technicians and engineering teams often possess detailed practical knowledge that managers may not have. Effective leadership requires respecting that expertise while maintaining accountability for standards and results.

Explain why a task matters, not just what needs to be done. Encourage technicians to report recurring faults, unsafe conditions, missing tools, poor-quality parts and unrealistic turnaround expectations without fear of being blamed for raising legitimate concerns.

Distinguish between a skills gap, a resource constraint, an unclear procedure and a performance problem. Each requires a different response.

For example, if battery repair output is low, investigate workload, diagnostic equipment, parts availability, repair complexity and quality requirements before concluding that technicians are underperforming.

Set clear expectations for safety, workmanship, documentation and escalation. Give constructive feedback, recognise good work and ensure that technical observations from the frontline reach the people who can act on them.

3. Navigating organisational dynamics: Understanding how decisions really happen

Organisational dynamics include formal reporting lines, informal influence, competing priorities, trust, reputation and the unwritten expectations that shape behaviour.

In one of operating countries for a multi-country company, these dynamics can be especially important. Some decisions may be made locally, while others require regional approval. Senior leaders may have different levels of visibility into local operating conditions, and teams may experience pressure to deliver expansion targets despite practical constraints.

A mid-level manager must understand these realities without becoming consumed by internal politics.

Understand authority, accountability and influence

  • Accountability: What outcome are you expected to deliver?
  • Authority: What decisions and resources are you authorised to control?
  • Influence: Which people must support or enable the outcome, even when they do not report to you?

These do not always align. You may be accountable for battery availability without directly managing charging, logistics or software. You may be expected to support motorcycle assembly without controlling procurement or supplier schedules.

When responsibility exceeds your authority, clarify decision rights and dependencies early. Agree on what you can decide, what requires consultation, what needs approval and how unresolved issues should be escalated.

Do not silently accept responsibility for outcomes that depend on decisions outside your control without making those dependencies visible. At the same time, avoid using limited authority as a reason for inaction. Identify the actions you can take, build support and escalate the specific decisions that remain outstanding.

Navigate the multi-country relationship professionally

The HQ company may establish strategic direction, budgets and expansion priorities, while the subsidiary country companies manages local execution and responds to local conditions.

The manager's role is to connect these perspectives. Regional leadership needs accurate information about what is happening locally; local teams need clarity about strategic expectations and approval requirements.

When a regional instruction encounters a local constraint, avoid presenting the issue as a conflict between headquarters and local context. Explain the operational reality, provide evidence and propose practical options.

For example, if CKD assembly targets are threatened by delayed imported components, communicate the affected assembly stages, stock position, expected lead times, operational consequences and available alternatives. This allows leadership to make an informed decision instead of receiving a late explanation for missed targets.

Respect reporting lines, but do not let them prevent necessary coordination. Where direct and indirect reporting relationships overlap, agree on priorities with the relevant managers and clarify who has final decision authority.

Build credibility through consistency

Organisational influence grows when people trust your judgement. That trust is built by delivering on commitments, sharing accurate information, acknowledging mistakes and giving credit fairly.

Avoid public blame, unnecessary confrontation and using informal conversations to undermine colleagues. If a disagreement concerns performance or responsibility, address it directly and professionally with the people involved.

It is also important to distinguish between facts and interpretations. A missed deadline is observable; the assumption that a colleague deliberately obstructed the work is not necessarily supported. Investigate the cause before drawing conclusions.

Organisational awareness is not manipulation. It is the ability to understand context, anticipate consequences and work constructively within the system while maintaining professional integrity.

4. Applying the three capabilities to a real operational challenge

Consider a situation where battery availability is declining despite a fleet of approximately 3,000 motorcycles, 26 swapping stations and 4,500 batteries.

A purely departmental response might be to instruct technicians to repair more batteries. A more effective manager investigates the entire battery operating cycle.

One challenge. Three management capabilities.

Strategic leadership identifies the business objective: increase usable battery availability while controlling operating costs and protecting battery life.

Stakeholder management brings together battery technicians, charging operations, station teams, logistics, software and engineering to establish where batteries are being lost from productive service.

Organisational awareness identifies who controls charging priorities, repair resources, battery movements, system data and expenditure approvals. It also clarifies which interventions the manager can implement independently and which require management approval.

The manager can then establish a baseline, identify the principal bottlenecks, assign responsibilities and review progress against agreed measures. This approach addresses the system rather than merely increasing activity in one department.

The same principles apply to CKD motorcycle assembly. Assembly performance depends not only on technician productivity but also on imported component availability, technical documentation, quality assurance, tooling, training, software configuration and coordination between procurement, engineering and operations.

The manager's value lies in making these dependencies visible and ensuring that the different functions work together towards a common delivery target.

5. A practical management discipline

Use the following questions when planning work, attending management meetings or resolving cross-functional problems.

  • What business outcome does this task support? Is the team improving fleet availability, safety, reliability, cost or expansion readiness?
  • What evidence distinguishes the root cause from the visible symptom?
  • Who needs to contribute, approve or be informed? Have responsibilities and deadlines been agreed?
  • Do I have the authority and resources to deliver? If not, which specific decision or dependency must be resolved?
  • What does the evidence show? Who owns the next action, when is it due, and how will we measure improvement?

Personal leadership discipline

These capabilities become stronger through deliberate habits. Think in systems, but communicate in clear decisions. Listen for the interest behind a position. Separate facts from assumptions and interpretations. Document agreements without turning documentation into a weapon. Give credit fairly, address performance issues directly and privately where appropriate, and remain calm when pressure rises. Protect ethical standards even when shortcuts appear attractive.

Finally, treat reflection as part of execution. After a significant decision or project, ask what created value, what was missed, whose perspective was absent, and which process should change. Seek feedback from people with different roles and incentives. Adaptability is not inconsistency; it is the discipline to learn and adjust while remaining anchored to principles and outcomes.

Conclusion: Become the link between strategy and execution

A successful mid-level manager does not need to have the highest technical authority or make every important decision. Their effectiveness comes from understanding the operating system, asking the right questions, building productive relationships and ensuring that responsibilities translate into results.

In a growing electric mobility company, this means connecting senior management's objectives with the realities faced by technicians, engineers and operational teams. It means recognising that battery availability, motorcycle reliability, station performance, software visibility and assembly readiness are interconnected business outcomes rather than isolated departmental responsibilities.

Strategic leadership helps the manager identify what matters most. Stakeholder management enables different people to contribute towards that objective. Organisational awareness helps the manager navigate authority, competing priorities and cross-functional dependencies without compromising integrity.

The defining skill of an effective mid-level manager is the ability to turn complex, cross-functional challenges into clear priorities, coordinated action and measurable business results.
